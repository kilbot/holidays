# Climate outlook: Australia, December 2026 – February 2027

Research pass for [issue #21](https://github.com/kilbot/holidays/issues/21). Verified against primary sources on **27 August 2026**.
Companion dataset: [`weather-normals.json`](./weather-normals.json).

---

## 1. The verdict up front

**The user's premise is correct — this time.** 2026–27 is genuinely shaping up as a very strong ("super") El Niño, and it is one of the most confidently forecast ENSO events on record. The Bureau of Meteorology, NOAA CPC and IRI all agree, with unusually little hedging.

**But the trade the premise implies is only half right.**

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "2026–27 is a super El Niño summer" | **Confirmed.** Very high confidence. |
| "Therefore a drier FNQ wet season" | **Directionally right, ~70% likely, concentrated in December–January.** |
| "Therefore January reef time is cheap *and* good" | **Cheap: yes, unconditionally. Good: a favourable bet, not a promise — and the edge dies in February.** |

The three things the premise misses:

1. **The El Niño dry signal in the tropics is a *wet-season-onset* effect, not a whole-summer effect.** BOM's own analysis: in El Niño years northern rainfall is "typically well-below-average during the early part of the wet season … but close to average during the latter part." The Port Douglas record bears this out precisely — January median rainfall in strong El Niño summers is **64% below normal**; February shows **no discount at all** (see §4).
2. **The two most recent strong El Niño summers both broke the bet.** 1997–98 (the closest "super" analogue) was Port Douglas's *wettest* strong-El-Niño summer on record. 2023–24 delivered ex-TC Jasper and Cairns's wettest of the composite set. A 6-of-8 hit rate is a good bet, not a sure one.
3. **The flip side lands squarely on Tasmania.** El Niño + a likely positive IOD + already-dry eastern Tasmania is the classic Australian bushfire setup, and AFAC has *already* flagged north-east Tasmania. The risk this outlook creates for the trip is not a rained-out reef day — it is a smoked-out or closed-park Tasmania capsule in early February.

## 2. Verified ENSO state

### Bureau of Meteorology — the official Australian position

**Source:** [Southern hemisphere monitoring / ENSO Wrap-Up](http://www.bom.gov.au/climate/enso/), **issued 11 August 2026** (current issue as at 27 Aug 2026).

> "Strong El Niño continues to intensify amid record-warm global sea-surface temperatures"

| Indicator | Value | Threshold | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relative Niño3.4 (week ending 9 Aug 2026) | **+2.20 °C** | +0.80 °C | Strong El Niño |
| SOI, 30-day to 8 Aug 2026 | **−25.6** | −7 | Deeply El Niño |
| SOI, July 2026 monthly average | **−29.1** | — | *Most negative July on record* |
| Equatorial upper-ocean heat content, July 2026 | *Highest on record* (since 1979) | — | More warming in the pipeline |
| Indian Ocean Dipole (9 Aug 2026) | +0.41 °C, **neutral** | +0.40 °C | On the threshold; positive IOD favoured to develop |
| Southern Annular Mode | Neutral | — | No signal |

BOM's forward view:

- Tropical Pacific continues warming through spring, **peaking in late spring or summer** — i.e. peaking *during the trip window*.
- Models point to a **very strong** event, likely to **peak above the warmest observed since 1950**.
- The event is expected to **persist into autumn 2027** — it will not have decayed before the travellers fly home.

BOM's own caveat, quoted verbatim because it is the single most important sentence in this document:

> "A strong El Niño signal in the Niño3.4 region does not necessarily mean strong impacts on Australia's climate; ENSO is only one of many factors that can influence Australia's seasonal weather and climate."

### NOAA Climate Prediction Center

**Source:** [ENSO Diagnostic Discussion](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/ensodisc.shtml), issued 13 August 2026.

- Alert System Status: **El Niño Advisory**.
- Synopsis: "El Niño is strengthening, with a greater than 90% chance of a very strong event during the Northern Hemisphere fall and winter 2026-27."
- October–December 2026: **69% chance of exceeding +2.5 °C** — which would make it the strongest event since at least 1950.

### IRI / Columbia

**Source:** [ENSO Quick Look](https://iri.columbia.edu/our-expertise/climate/forecasts/enso/current/), issued 19 August 2026.

- El Niño probability: **100% for Nov-Dec-Jan, Dec-Jan-Feb and Jan-Feb-Mar.**
- Niño3.4 forecast to remain above +2.0 °C through DJF; 15 models reach or exceed +3.0 °C in Oct-Nov-Dec.

**Three independent agencies, no disagreement.** Confidence in the *phase*: **very high**. This is about as certain as seasonal climate prediction gets.

### Cross-check: is "super El Niño" the right label?

Yes. "Super El Niño" is journalism rather than a BOM classification — BOM says "strong" and "very strong" — but the substance holds. Peak Niño3.4 forecast above anything observed since 1950 puts this in the 1982–83 / 1997–98 / 2015–16 tier or beyond. **No correction to the user's premise is required on the ENSO phase itself.**

## 3. What the official *outlook* actually says — and what it doesn't

### The honest gap

**BOM has not yet issued a December–February forecast.** Its ACCESS-S model runs four months ahead, so the current issue ([20 August 2026](http://www.bom.gov.au/climate/ahead/outlooks/)) is titled "Climate outlook for September to December" and details **September–November only**.

**A Dec–Feb outlook will not exist until roughly mid-November 2026.** Everything in this document about the trip window itself is either (a) BOM's general El Niño impact statistics, or (b) my own historical-analogue analysis of BOM station data. Neither is a forecast. This is the central uncertainty and no amount of confidence about the ENSO phase removes it.

### BOM long-range forecast, issued 20 August 2026 (Sep–Nov)

**Rainfall**
- Below average (60% to >80%): far south-west WA, **south-eastern Australia including Tasmania**, and parts of eastern and northern Queensland.
- Increased chance of *unusually low* rainfall (50–70%) across parts of south-eastern Australia, on top of two years of existing deficiency.
- **Above average (60% to >80%) over most of Western Australia**, extending into the NT and western SA/Qld — with unusually high rainfall 50–80% likely across northern and central WA.
- "The dry signal is strongest early in the season … southern Victoria and Tasmania remain drier than average forecast throughout the season."

**Temperature**
- Maximum temperatures above average (60% to >80%) across most of Australia **except parts of the tropical north**.
- Notably: maximum temperatures **below average (60–80%) across areas of north-eastern Queensland** and the northern Kimberley.
- Unusually high maxima >80% likely across most of south-western and south-eastern Australia.

Two things here are worth flagging because they cut against the naive story:

- **WA is forecast *wetter*, not drier.** This is an Indian Ocean effect, not a Pacific one. A strong El Niño does not dry out Western Australia.
- **North-east Queensland is forecast *cooler* than average.** Below-average maxima in the tropics usually mean cloud and rain, not clear skies. That is a mild warning shot against assuming a blue-sky FNQ wet season.

### BOM Northern Rainfall Onset, issued 13 August 2026

**Source:** [Northern rainfall onset](http://www.bom.gov.au/climate/rainfall-onset/)

> "Northern and eastern Queensland and the Top End of the Northern Territory have a **55–80% chance of a later than normal northern rainfall onset**."

BOM adds that this is the season's final and highest-accuracy issue. This is the strongest *forecast* (as opposed to analogue) support for the user's bet: the FNQ wet season is more likely than not to start late. BOM's general statistics put the El Niño monsoon-onset delay at **2–6 weeks**.

A 2–6 week delay on a normal late-December/early-January monsoon onset lands the disrupted period across December and the first half of January — and pushes the *back half* of January towards a normal wet season. The reef window of **18–31 January** sits right on the boundary.

## 4. Historical analogue analysis (my own, from BOM station data)

BOM publishes El Niño composites as maps, not numbers, so I pulled the raw monthly rainfall series from BOM Climate Data Online and computed the composites directly.

**Method.** Strong-to-very-strong El Niño summers, keyed by the December of the event: 1957, 1965, 1972, 1982, 1987, 1991, 1997, 2015, 2023. (There is no single canonical BOM "strong" list; this is the conventional ONI ≥ ~1.5 set, and 1987 and 1991 sit at the moderate-to-strong edge.) Compared against the all-year median for the full station record. Sample sizes are 6–9 events — **statistically thin, and every number below should be read with that in mind.**

### Port Douglas — Warner St (station 031052, rainfall record 1884–2020)

| | Dec | Jan | Feb | DJF total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| All-year median (mm) | 136 | **364** | 383 | 1017 |
| Strong El Niño median (mm) | 82 | **132** | 386 | 659 |
| Change | −40% | **−64%** | **0%** | −35% |

5 of 6 strong El Niño summers came in below the all-year DJF median. The exception — **1997–98, the closest "super" analogue — delivered 1601 mm**, more than half again the normal summer.

Individual strong-El-Niño Januaries at Port Douglas: 249, 165, 98, 64, 62, 409 mm.

### Cairns Aero (station 031011, 1943–2025)

| | Dec | Jan | Feb | DJF total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| All-year median (mm) | 130 | 355 | 396 | 926 |
| Strong El Niño median (mm) | 215 | 196 | 217 | 567 |
| Change | +65% | **−45%** | −45% | **−39%** |

6 of 8 below median. The two failures are the two most recent events: 1997–98 (1038 mm) and 2023–24 (1282 mm — ex-TC Jasper).

**The two FNQ stations disagree about February, and that disagreement is itself informative.** Port Douglas shows no February discount; Cairns shows −45%. The event sets are not identical — the Port Douglas monthly series I pulled runs 1884–2020 (so it includes 1957–58 but not 2015–16 or 2023–24), while Cairns runs 1943–2025 (the reverse). With six to eight events per station, one or two swapped years moves the median a long way. Treat the Port Douglas figure as the more relevant one for the trip (it is the actual base and has the longer rainfall record), treat the direction as "the January signal is much stronger than the February signal," and do not treat either February number as precise.

### Hobart, Ellerslie Road (station 094029, 1882–2025)

| | Dec | Jan | Feb | DJF total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| All-year median (mm) | 47 | 36 | 31 | 135 |
| Strong El Niño median (mm) | 36 | 28 | 22 | **98** |
| Change | −23% | −22% | −29% | **−27%** |

**7 of 9 below median.** The most consistent signal of any location analysed. A drier-than-normal Tasmanian summer is the single most reliable El Niño consequence for this itinerary — and it is the *bad* one.

### Sydney, Observatory Hill (station 066062, 1859–2019)

| | Dec | Jan | Feb | DJF total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| All-year median (mm) | 60 | 80 | 91 | 262 |
| Strong El Niño median (mm) | 57 | 114 | 121 | **416** |
| Change | −5% | +43% | +33% | **+59%** |

**Only 3 of 8 below median.** The El Niño dry signal at Sydney is a winter–spring phenomenon that has essentially decayed by summer. BOM/ABC put the seasonal decay at roughly −32% in winter, −20% in spring, **−2% in summer**.

**Conclusion: El Niño gives you no usable edge on Sydney NYE weather.** Use the raw base rates instead (§5).

### Perth Metro (station 009225, 1994–2024)

December median rainfall is **4.4 mm** across 1.8 rain days. There is nothing for El Niño to subtract. WA December is about heat, not rain — and BOM's current outlook actually favours a *wetter* WA spring.

## 5. What this phase means, region by region

### FNQ wet season and tropical cyclones

**Cyclones — the strongest and most useful signal.** BOM's official position ([El Niño and Australia](http://www.bom.gov.au/climate/updates/articles/a008-el-nino-and-australia.shtml)):

> "On average, there are fewer tropical cyclones in the Australian region during El Niño years. This is particularly true around Queensland, where **cyclones are half as likely to cross the coast during El Niño years compared to neutral years**."

Baseline ([BOM cyclone climatology](http://www.bom.gov.au/climate/cyclones/australia/)): ~10 TCs per Australian season since 1980–81 (8–9 since 2000), 3–4 crossing the coast. **The fewest ever recorded was 3, in 2015–16 — a very strong El Niño year.** The most was 18 in 1983–84, immediately after the 1982–83 El Niño, which is a reminder that the relationship is a tilt, not a rule.

**Trade winds.** BOM reports trade winds "either reversed or weaker than average across the western tropical Pacific." Reef trips are cancelled by *wind*, not rain — a weaker south-easterly regime in the Coral Sea plausibly means fewer blown-out reef days. This is inference rather than a published forecast; treat as **moderate confidence**, but it points the right way.

**The counterweight nobody wants to hear: coral bleaching.** A very strong El Niño combined with record-warm global SSTs is the textbook precursor to mass bleaching, and the reef entered this season already degraded after a sixth mass bleaching event since 2016. Multiple outlets report scientists warning of high bleaching risk on the GBR through early 2027. Two honest qualifications: (a) these are warnings in secondary reporting, not a BOM or GBRMPA forecast; (b) BOM's own SST analysis (11 Aug 2026) records waters "near average … to most of the north" of Australia even while the south-east runs 2–3 °C hot. **The bleaching risk is real but not yet realised.** Re-check NOAA Coral Reef Watch and GBRMPA reef health updates in November and December.

Net: a drier, calmer, less cyclone-prone reef window is a genuinely better *logistics* bet. Whether it is a better *reef* is a separate question with a worse answer.

### Tasmania

The flip side, and the part of the itinerary this outlook actually damages.

- Rainfall composite: **DJF 27% below median, 7 of 9 strong El Niño summers below median** — the most consistent signal in this analysis.
- BOM's Sep–Nov outlook already forecasts below-average rainfall for Tasmania, and says "southern Victoria and Tasmania remain drier than average forecast throughout the season."
- BOM lists "increased fire danger in southeast Australia" as a standard El Niño consequence, **"particularly when combined with a positive Indian Ocean Dipole"** — which models favour developing. This is the exact combination that preceded Ash Wednesday (Feb 1983) and the 2002–03 and 2006–07 fire seasons.
- **AFAC's Seasonal Bushfire Outlook for Spring 2026 (released 19 August 2026) already flags increased fire risk for north-east and eastern Tasmania**, driven by below-average rainfall.

An early-February Tasmania capsule sits at the back end of the fire season in a year loaded for fire. The practical risks are smoke haze over the Tarkine/Cradle Mountain, walking-track and national-park closures, and total fire bans killing campfire-based plans. Note that BOM's *coastal water* forecast is the compensating upside: eastern and southern Tasmanian waters are running **2–3 °C above average**, which makes east-coast swimming and Bay of Fires beach time unusually pleasant.

The one thing El Niño does *not* do to Tasmania is make it hot — Hobart's normal January maximum is 21.8 °C with 1.9 days above 30 °C. Expect a dry, bright, breezy, mild summer, with fire as the tail risk.

### Western Australia — December

Heat, not rain, is the variable. Perth Metro December normals: 29.6 °C mean maximum, **13.2 days above 30 °C and 5.4 days above 35 °C**. January is worse (18.3 and 7.7). El Niño years tend to bring hotter individual daily extremes to southern Australia even though prolonged warm spells are *less* frequent (more mobile weather systems, fewer blocking highs).

Rainfall is a non-issue: December median 4.4 mm. And BOM's current outlook favours **above-average** rainfall across most of WA through spring, which is an Indian Ocean signal — a useful corrective to "El Niño means dry everywhere."

Margaret River runs 3–5 °C cooler than Perth (December mean max 24.9 °C) but noticeably windier (mean 3 pm wind 22 km/h). Bushfire risk in the south-west is real but driven by fuel load and soil dryness, both of which AFAC flags for parts of WA.

### East coast — Sydney, Byron

No usable El Niño rainfall edge in summer (§4). Warmer than normal is likely; drier is not. BOM notes coastal waters off southern NSW are running **2–3 °C above average**, so ocean swimming should be warm.

Byron's Cape Byron record is too short (2004–) for a meaningful composite, and February is its wettest month by a clear margin (mean 200 mm, 12.1 rain days ≥1 mm) — that is normal climatology, not an El Niño effect. Byron sits outside the cyclone zone but remains exposed to ex-tropical-cyclone rain events and east-coast lows.

## 6. The weather bet, per weather-sensitive capsule

### GBR / FNQ — the arbitrage verdict

**Confidence: moderate. The bet is worth taking; the framing needs fixing.**

Split the arbitrage in two, because the two halves have completely different risk profiles:

- **The cost half is unconditional.** January is FNQ low season *because* of the wet season, stingers and cyclones — the discount exists whether or not the weather cooperates. The travellers capture it either way.
- **The weather half is a ~70% probability, not a fact.** The odds: 5 of 6 strong El Niño summers below-median at Port Douglas, 6 of 8 at Cairns, with a January-specific discount of −64% (Port Douglas) to −45% (Cairns) against the normal median. That is a good bet. It is not a guarantee, and the two most recent strong events (1997–98, 2023–24) were both busts.

**The load-bearing detail: the edge is in December and January and vanishes in February.** Port Douglas February in strong El Niño summers: 386 mm against a 383 mm normal — *zero* discount. This is BOM's own documented mechanism (delayed onset, near-normal late wet season), not a statistical fluke.

**Action:** hold the reef window at 18–31 January and resist any slippage into February. If the itinerary pressure is to push the reef later, that pressure is destroying the entire premise of the arbitrage. If anything, pulling the window earlier in January strengthens it.

**What the bet buys, honestly:**
- ✅ ~70% chance of a drier-than-normal January (real, sizeable effect)
- ✅ Materially lower cyclone risk — Queensland coastal crossings roughly halved; fewest-ever Australian season (3) was a very strong El Niño
- ✅ Probably fewer wind-cancelled reef days (weaker trades; moderate confidence)
- ✅ Guaranteed off-peak pricing regardless of outcome
- ⚠️ Still a tropical wet season. Even a 45% discount on a Port Douglas January leaves ~190 mm across ~8 rain days. "Drier" means fewer washouts, not a dry holiday.
- ⚠️ No relief from heat or humidity: 30 °C daily maxima, 78% 9 am humidity, unchanged by ENSO
- ⚠️ Stinger season regardless — 1 Nov to 31 May, peak Dec–Mar. Suits mandatory.
- ❌ **Elevated coral bleaching risk** — the one way this trade can be right about the weather and still lose. Re-check in Nov/Dec.

**Contingency:** book reef days with operators offering weather-reschedule policies, and keep at least one buffer day in the FNQ block so a blown-out reef day can be re-run rather than lost.

### Tasmania (early February)

**Confidence: moderate-high that it will be dry and mild; moderate that fire becomes a live disruption.**

The most statistically reliable El Niño signal in this whole analysis points at Tasmania, and it points the wrong way for the trip. Expect a dry, bright, breezy summer — pleasant on its face, with warm east-coast water (BOM: 2–3 °C above average) — carrying a materially elevated risk of bushfire smoke, track closures and total fire bans. AFAC has already flagged north-east and eastern Tasmania for spring; the summer outlook lands late November.

**Action:** treat Tasmania as the capsule that needs a Plan B, not the reef. Favour coastal and east-coast plans (Freycinet, Bay of Fires, Maria Island) over the interior wilderness walks that close first when fire danger spikes. Do not commit non-refundable money to Overland Track or Cradle Mountain multi-day walking until the AFAC Summer outlook (late November) and the Tasmania Fire Service picture in January. Keep the capsule genuinely toggleable — this is exactly the situation the capsule model exists for.

### Byron Bay (January/February)

**Confidence: low that ENSO tells you anything useful here.**

No dependable El Niño summer signal on the northern NSW coast. Plan against the raw normals: January mean max 28.1 °C, ~150 mm across 10.5 rain days; February wetter at ~200 mm across 12.1 days. Warm ocean (approx. 25–26 °C) is a near-certainty and likely above normal.

**Action:** if there is a choice between a January and a February Byron slot, **January is the drier one** — that is straight climatology and has nothing to do with El Niño. Do not let a "super El Niño means dry" heuristic talk anyone into February.

### Sydney harbour NYE

**Confidence: high — in the finding that El Niño gives you no edge.**

Sydney's strong-El-Niño DJF composite is *wetter* than the all-year median (416 mm vs 262 mm), with only 3 of 8 events below median. Plan against the raw December base rates from 162 years at Observatory Hill:

| Outcome on any given December day | Probability |
|---|---|
| Any measurable rain | **37%** (11.5 days / 31) |
| ≥1 mm — a genuinely wet day | **25%** (7.9 / 31) |
| ≥10 mm — evening plans actually ruined | **7%** (2.2 / 31) |

**About a 1-in-4 chance of a damp NYE and a 1-in-14 chance of a properly wet one.** Sydney fireworks proceed in rain; the risk is comfort and visibility, not cancellation. Conditions on the night: ~25 °C max, minimum ~18 °C, 59% 3 pm humidity — warm, muggy, fine for an outdoor night.

**Action:** no weather-driven planning change. Do not pay a premium for a "rain-safe" vantage point on a 7% risk; do carry a light rain shell.

### Perth / Margaret River (December)

**Confidence: high on hot and dry; moderate on how hot.**

December in Perth is functionally rain-free (median 4.4 mm) with 5.4 days above 35 °C, and El Niño tilts individual daily extremes hotter. Margaret River is a 3–5 °C cooler, windier alternative.

**Action:** plan around heat, not rain — early starts for anything active (Rottnest cycling in particular), Margaret River or the coast as the heat escape. No weather-driven change to the capsule.

## 8. Honest uncertainty

Read the rest of this document through these five caveats.

1. **BOM has issued no Dec–Feb forecast yet.** Everything specific to the trip window is analogue reasoning from past events, not prediction. That changes around mid-November.
2. **Strength does not equal impact.** BOM: "the strength of an El Niño is not directly proportional to the rainfall impacts." Their own example: the very strong 1997–98 event produced rainfall impacts "generally confined to coastal southeastern Australia and Tasmania," while the *weak* 2002–03 event produced widespread severe drought.
3. **The samples are small.** Six to nine events per composite. A single outlier event moves the median. The Port Douglas January figure rests on six data points.
4. **The teleconnection may be weakening.** Reporting on BOM analysis (ABC, 22 Aug 2026) notes El Niño's Australian rainfall suppression decaying from ~−32% in winter to ~−20% in spring to ~−2% in summer, with a warming Indian Ocean supplying extra atmospheric moisture — BOM climatologist Zhi-Weng Chua: "Warmer waters can increase atmospheric moisture, providing favourable conditions for rainfall-producing weather systems." A hot-Indian-Ocean, climate-changed El Niño may not behave like its 20th-century analogues. **Australia has been wet so far during this El Niño**, which is exactly the signal that should keep confidence at "moderate."
5. **Sea surface temperatures in `weather-normals.json` are approximate.** BOM does not publish tabular monthly SST normals by town. Those values are indicative regional climatology (±1.5 °C), flagged as such in the file, and should be replaced with a live source before the site presents them as data.

## 9. Design note: the three-layer weather model

For surfacing "what will it be like *that week, in that place*" on the site.

### Layer 1 — Normals (static)

`docs/research/weather-normals.json`. BOM climate statistics for 11 bases, Dec/Jan/Feb: mean max/min, mean and **median** rainfall, rain days ≥1 mm and ≥10 mm, days ≥30 °C and ≥35 °C, 9 am/3 pm humidity, 3 pm wind, approximate SST, plus stinger/cyclone/bushfire flags.

Never changes. Ships with the repo. No network call.

Two implementation notes:
- **Show median rainfall, not mean.** Tropical means are inflated by rare extreme months — Port Douglas December is 213 mm mean but 137 mm median. The median is what a typical month actually delivers.
- **Rain *days*, not millimetres, are what a traveller feels.** `rain_days_ge_1mm / days_in_month` is a wet-day probability and reads far better in a UI than a total. Monthly normals interpolate acceptably to a week; genuine weekly normals would need daily-series reprocessing and are not worth it at this precision.

### Layer 2 — Seasonal tilt (re-check monthly)

The `enso_tilt` block in the same file: per-location direction (drier/wetter/hotter), a confidence rating, and the evidence. Rendered as an annotation over Layer 1 — *"normally 342 mm across 14 rain days; this year tilted drier (moderate confidence, very strong El Niño)"* — never as an adjusted number. Adjusting the number implies precision that does not exist.

Hand-maintained, refreshed on the §7 schedule, with an `as_at` date shown in the UI so a stale tilt is visible as stale.

### Layer 3 — Live forecast (future site feature, ~10-day horizon only)

Only meaningful inside about ten days, so it never applies at planning time and always applies during the trip. Should visibly *replace* Layers 1–2 for dates in range rather than sitting alongside them.

**API options:**

- **[Open-Meteo](https://open-meteo.com)** — the pragmatic choice. Free for non-commercial use, no API key, CORS-enabled so it can be called straight from the browser. Verified working during this research:
  - Forecast: `https://api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast?latitude=-16.48&longitude=145.46&daily=temperature_2m_max,precipitation_sum,precipitation_probability_max&timezone=Australia/Brisbane`
  - **Marine** (`https://marine-api.open-meteo.com/v1/marine`) — wave height and sea surface temperature. Genuinely the highest-value endpoint here: reef trips are cancelled by swell and wind, not rain, so wave height is a better reef-day predictor than any rainfall figure.
  - Historical/ERA5 archive API also available if weekly normals are ever wanted from real daily data rather than monthly tables.
- **BOM** — no public JSON API. The available route is anonymous FTP XML (`ftp.bom.gov.au/anon/gen/fwo/`, e.g. IDQ11295 for Queensland precis forecasts), which needs a server-side fetch-and-parse job plus caching, and BOM's terms require attribution and discourage frequent polling. Authoritative for Australia and the right long-term answer, but not a browser-callable API.
- **Recommendation:** Open-Meteo for the live layer, with BOM cited as the source of Layers 1 and 2, and BOM warnings/marine forecasts linked out to rather than scraped.
